# Marketing Positioning: Eve Sorts Out Your Life

This document translates user research into outward-facing positioning and IA guidance. It deliberately avoids
technical AI-agent vocabulary unless a user asks how Eve works.

## Lead direction

The strongest current plain-language proposition is the current HTML-tour hero:

> **Bring Eve the mess. Leave with less to do.**

The longer supporting proposition below carries the same relief-and-goals idea, but should not replace
the simpler front-door line.

> **Eve sorts out your life for you, for free, so you have time for your goals.**

Why it works:

- **“sorts out your life”** describes the felt outcome instead of the mechanism;
- **“for you”** makes delegation explicit rather than implying another advice tool;
- **“so you have time for your goals”** gives the relief a purpose: Eve is not optimizing life-admin
  for its own sake, but protecting the things the user actually wants to do.

The phrase **“for free” is a factual product/pricing claim, not merely tone**. Use it only in markets,
builds, and distribution paths where the actual user experience can truthfully support it without a
hidden required subscription or usage charge. The underlying positioning remains useful without it:

> **Eve sorts out your life for you, so you have time for your goals.**

## The positioning problem

“Desktop agent,” “automation,” “persistent context,” “computer use,” “workers,” and “permissions”
may all be technically accurate, but they do not tell an ordinary person why Eve matters.

Most target users already understand that a chatbot can answer questions. Eve needs to make the
additional value immediately legible:

> **Bring Eve the problem. She should leave you with less to do.**

The marketing distinction is not “smarter answers.” It is movement from advice toward practical
relief.

## Positioning and IA guardrail

The front door should answer four questions in this order:

1. **What recognizable problem can I bring Eve?** Start with ordinary-life friction: the broken app,
   the university pile-up, recurring admin, a decision, clutter, or something the person keeps putting
   off.
2. **What will feel lighter afterward?** Show what Eve can prepare, carry, check, organize, or finish
   so the user gets fewer loose ends and a smaller decision surface.
3. **Why should I rely on the result?** Explain the observable product behavior in plain language:
   the user chooses what Eve may see and change, one main conversation coordinates the work, useful
   state survives interruptions, and finished work stays distinguishable from guesses or activity.
4. **How does it work?** Only after the first three should the IA introduce local tools, workers,
   Plans, Threads, Quilts, model choices, connectors, or other mechanism vocabulary.

This ordering also separates durable product differentiation from familiar agent-marketing veneer:

| Product behavior to prove | Language that cannot carry the claim by itself |
| --- | --- |
| Eve adds practical local continuity and action around the ChatGPT conversation pattern people already know. | “A new AI assistant,” “agent OS,” or a different chatbot personality. |
| One coordinating conversation can hand off focused parts of a larger job and bring the useful result back without making the user manage every thread. | “Multi-agent,” “AI workforce,” “parallel agents,” or impressive worker counts. |
| Local project state, history, Plans, queued work, and exact ownership let the same work continue after interruptions or a too-long provider chat. | “Persistent memory,” “always on,” or “works while you sleep” as abstract slogans. |
| Explicit permissions, source and ownership evidence, truthful completion state, and recovery rules make action inspectable and controllable. | “Secure,” “private,” “safe,” or “local-first” without showing the actual control or evidence. |
| Eve can use the user's computer and connected tools when authorized, so some problems can move beyond advice into completed work. | “Autonomous,” “hands free,” or broad capability superlatives. |

Technical depth matters because it substantiates these product truths. It should not become the
front-door identity. Competitors can copy words like “agent,” “autonomous,” “local,” and “multi-agent”
quickly; the durable brand advantage is the experience of handing over an ordinary problem, seeing
what Eve is doing and allowed to do, and getting back less work to carry.

## Core service promise

The clearest internal test for product and copy is:

> **Don’t hand the problem back to me.**

Supporting lines worth testing:

> **Less advice. More handled.**

> **Bring Eve the mess. Keep only the decisions.**

> **Tell Eve what’s bothering you. She’ll see what she can take care of.**

> **Ask for an answer, or give Eve something to handle.**

The first line is the product principle; the others are possible campaign, onboarding, or discovery
copy rather than settled slogans.

## What Eve is selling emotionally

Do not lead with time saved, agents launched, or tasks automated. The stronger emotional outcomes
are:

- “I don’t have to figure this out again.”
- “I don’t have to remember to check.”
- “I don’t have to do all the boring parts myself.”
- “I can keep doing what I was doing.”
- “I only need to make the decisions that are actually mine.”
- “I have more room for university, family, hobbies, relationships, rest, and long-term goals.”

The user is buying relief from **figuring it out, remembering it, starting it, checking it, sorting
it, researching it, and worrying that something was forgotten**.

## The Donna-style character promise

Eve should feel like a combination of:

- a super-secretary who handles practical details;
- a coach who helps the user move instead of merely admire a plan;
- a mentor who improves decisions;
- a warm, funny co-conspirator the user is happy to tell the messy version to.

This should never be marketed as omniscience. The useful promise is behavioral: Eve notices the
real friction, handles what she safely can, and brings back the smallest possible decision surface.

The ideal Eve update is not:

> “Here are twelve steps you can take.”

It is closer to:

> “I handled the first eleven. Which of these two do you want?”

## Discovery copy should start with life, not features

Good entry points:

- **I’m starting university**
- **My computer is a mess**
- **Something isn’t working**
- **I’m overwhelmed with little things**
- **I need to make an important decision**
- **There’s something I keep putting off**
- **I want more time for a goal**
- **Show me what Eve could take off my plate**

Bad entry points for first-use marketing:

- Automations
- Agents
- Persistent context
- Background execution
- Computer permissions
- Multi-agent workflows

Those may exist under the hood, but they force the user to learn the product before imagining the
benefit.

## Explain capabilities in ordinary language

When explanation is necessary, prefer:

| Avoid leading with | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| persistent context | “Eve remembers what you were working on.” |
| automation | “Eve can keep an eye on this for you.” |
| computer use | “Eve can do this on your computer.” |
| permissions | “You choose what Eve is allowed to see and change.” |
| agents / workers | “Eve can split up a big job and work on several parts.” |
| asynchronous execution | “Eve can keep working while you do something else.” |

## Eight showcase promises

The product should demonstrate concrete delegation rather than an automation gallery:

1. **Keep me on top of university.**
2. **Help me catch up when I’ve fallen behind.**
3. **Sort out this mess on my laptop.**
4. **Something’s broken. Help me fix it.**
5. **Tell me what I actually need to deal with this week.**
6. **Figure out what I should choose.**
7. **Take this annoying recurring chore off my mind.**
8. **Help me actually finish this goal.**

Each promise should resolve into visible work Eve can carry, not another list of tips.

## Competitive framing

Do not base the brand on claims that another assistant categorically cannot perform a particular
action. Large AI products will keep absorbing more tools and action capabilities.

The durable distinction should be the experience:

> **Eve is the assistant that tries to take the annoying work off your hands instead of turning it
> into better-organized homework.**

The product wins when a user who already uses ChatGPT daily discovers a new reflex:

> “Maybe I can just give this to Eve.”

## Audience guardrail: do not market Eve as another automation stack

Developer forums, founder communities, Product Hunt, indie-hacker circles, and discount-software
marketplaces contain an active audience hunting for cheaper automation, self-hosted agent stacks, and
marketing tools. They can be valuable early adopters, but they are not the clearest expression of the
consumer brand.

Avoid making the front door sound like:

- “Build powerful automations for less.”
- “Replace your SaaS stack.”
- “Run multi-agent workflows locally.”
- “Get enterprise automation without enterprise pricing.”

Those messages attract people who already know what they want to automate. Eve's broader opportunity
is with people who **do not know what could be delegated yet**.

Prefer:

> **You already have enough to manage. Eve helps take some of it off your hands.**

The technical depth can remain available underneath. The consumer promise should stay focused on
relief, practical help, and time protected for the user's own goals.

### Learn from OpenClaw without copying its audience

OpenClaw's viral appeal came from a very legible promise: the AI could reach into real tools and
carry a chain of work rather than stopping at advice. Its most visible stories often centered on
solopreneurs, autonomous marketing teams, inbox/CRM operations, recurring research, and the dream of
running more with fewer employees.

Eve should keep the visceral part of that promise and change the audience:

> **OpenClaw made “AI that does the work” exciting for power users. Eve should make that feeling
> obvious for ordinary people.**

Do not sell “multi-agent orchestration,” “autonomous pipelines,” or “no-employee operations” on the
front door. Show the same capability as relief:

- “Keep me from falling behind at university.”
- “Sort this mess out for me.”
- “Find out why this keeps crashing and fix what you safely can.”
- “Keep an eye on this so I don't have to.”
- “Handle the boring parts and bring me the decisions.”

The competitive target is not maximum visible activity. The target is **maximum amount of annoyance
removed per thing the user still has to think about**.

## Copy quality test

Before publishing consumer copy, ask:

1. Could a tired nontechnical person understand the benefit without knowing an AI term?
2. Does the copy describe something they already recognize in their life?
3. Does Eve appear to reduce work, or merely generate instructions for more work?
4. Is the promised action something the current product can truthfully do?
5. Does the result protect time or attention for something the user actually values?

If the copy requires explaining what “persistent,” “agentic,” “automation,” or “context” means, it
probably belongs under “How Eve works,” not on the front door.

## Competitive lesson: make improvement and trust visible

OpenClaw-style products created excitement around an AI that can actually take actions. Hermes-style
switching stories add another useful marketing lesson: users talk when the product seems to **learn
them**, feels **safer to trust**, and makes **switching painless**.

Do not market those ideas with developer language such as “closed learning loops,” “namespaces,” or
“migration tooling.” Translate them into ordinary promises:

- **“Eve remembers what worked last time.”**
- **“You can see what Eve is doing and stop her when you want.”**
- **“Bring your existing work with you; you should not have to start over.”**
- **“The more Eve helps with your real life, the less explaining you should have to repeat.”**

These are supporting reasons to believe the main promise, not the headline. The headline should
still be about relief and goals, for example:

> **Eve sorts out your life for you, so you have time for your goals.**

Competitive claims about another product's market share, security incidents, malware prevalence, or
viral reach must be verified from primary or high-quality sources before ParadigmEve repeats them in
public copy.
